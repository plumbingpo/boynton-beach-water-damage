Of all the stages in restoring water damage, drying the structure counts most, and it is the stage most often botched by people without training. Extraction takes care of standing water, but drywall, framing, subflooring, insulation, concrete and other building materials stay loaded with trapped moisture. A structure left wet ends up with odors, wood rot, mold and failing materials, so the moisture has to be removed in a scientific way using dehumidification and controlled evaporation.

The structural drying technicians at Boynton Beach Restoration are IICRC-certified and trained in psychrometry, the science of how temperature, humidity and moisture in air relate. That knowledge lets them work out where equipment goes, what temperature to hold and how air should move so evaporation is as effective as possible on every job. Because hardwood, drywall, plaster and concrete do not dry at the same speed, the method has to change with the material to bring it to acceptable moisture content without harming it further.

Our structural drying process includes daily moisture monitoring with calibrated moisture meters, thermo-hygrometers and thermal imaging cameras. Readings are recorded from start to finish in a detailed drying log, which shows moisture dropping step by step in every affected material. Equipment is adjusted daily based on these readings to optimize drying efficiency. Drying is complete only when all affected materials reach acceptable moisture standards as defined by IICRC S500 guidelines, not when surfaces simply feel dry to the touch.

Structural Drying Prevention Tips

A little prevention goes a long way in avoiding expensive restoration emergencies. Follow these tips from our IICRC-certified technicians:

  • Do not place personal items on top of drying equipment or block the airflow from air movers
  • Go by the meter, not by touch, since a dry-feeling surface may still hold moisture deep inside
  • Avoid moving the machines yourself, because their placement follows our moisture map
  • New odors or wet areas during the drying period should be reported to your technician right away
  • Address any water intrusion immediately, because the faster drying begins, the better the outcome
  • Let air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, 24/7, until the technician removes them

The Benefits of Professional Structural Drying

Structural drying is a science, not a guessing game. The leading cause of secondary mold after a water event is setting out a few fans and trusting they will be enough. It involves daily monitoring, a calculation of the required grain depression (the capacity to remove moisture) and a working knowledge of the way materials absorb moisture and let it go. Get it wrong in one direction and mold feeds on leftover moisture, get it wrong in the other and wood or similar materials are damaged by over-drying.

Professional drying equipment is not a bigger version of what stores sell, it is a different kind of machine. A commercial LGR dehumidifier, short for low-grain refrigerant, pulls 10 to 20 times as much moisture out of the air as a residential one, while high-velocity air movers set up the exact airflow that speeds evaporation off structural surfaces. Between the investment in equipment and the training needed to run it, it is no surprise that DIY drying falls well short of a professional job.

Signs It Is Time for Structural Drying

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • High humidity in affected areas despite efforts to ventilate
  • Surfaces that stay cool or clammy once the standing water has been removed
  • Baseboards or door trim that swell, warp or pull away from the wall
  • Gaps opening up in a hardwood floor, or planks with raised edges or centers
  • Trapped moisture often announces itself with a persistent musty odor after cleanup
  • Materials breaking down in a spot that got wet before and was not dried by professionals
  • Ceilings, floors or walls that still read high on a moisture meter following a leak
